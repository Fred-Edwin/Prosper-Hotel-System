## 2026-08-05 — Initial discovery, Lucy

**Client:** Lucy (WaPrecious), owner, Prosper Hotel
**Prepared by:** Edwin
**Purpose:** Record how Prosper Hotel actually operates, in the owner's own terms, as the basis for designing a system to run it.

> This document describes the business, not a solution. It deliberately contains no screens, tables, or technology. A reader should finish it understanding how Prosper Hotel works well enough to propose a system — without having been told what that system is.

---

## 1. The business

Prosper Hotel is a small, owner-operated hospitality business in Kenya. Lucy owns it and makes every commercial decision — what's sold, what it costs, what it sells for, who works where. She has five people including herself, across two locations.

The most important thing to understand about Lucy's involvement: **she holds a day job and is not usually at the business during working hours.** She runs it around her employment — checking in, reviewing figures, and collecting money. But she is there in person often enough, and unpredictably enough, that this matters: when she is on site, she needs to be able to do *anything* her staff can do. Serve a customer, take an order, receive a delivery from a supplier, record what was sent to the canteen. She is not only an observer of her business; she is an occasional operator of every position in it.

This is a small business with genuinely real accounting needs — not a chain, and not a hobby.

---

## 2. The two locations

### 2.1 The restaurant

A small sit-in restaurant with a kitchen. This is the operational centre of the business. It holds the **main store**, which is where goods arrive from suppliers and where raw ingredients are kept before being cooked.

It sells cooked food and drinks — stews, mukimo, githeri, chips, rice, teas and coffees, mandazi, chapati, samosa, eggs, smokies. It also runs a **printing station**, selling photocopying, printing, binding, research and official forms alongside the food. And it carries takeaway packaging — containers, envelopes, cups, disposable cutlery.

### 2.2 The university canteen

A second, smaller outlet located inside a university, serving students.

Commercially it behaves much more like a **retail shop than a restaurant.** Its trade is dominated by packaged and ready-to-sell goods: bottled drinks and sodas, biscuits, sweets, crisps and snacks, fruit, stationery (exercise books, foolscaps, pens, sharpeners), over-the-counter remedies such as painkillers and ENO, and general items like handkerchiefs, masks, sanitary products, water containers and **airtime**. It has its own cyber corner offering photocopying, printing and binding. Only a small part of what it sells is cooked food.

Treating the canteen as "a smaller version of the restaurant" would misread it. It is a shop that happens to also sell some food from the restaurant's kitchen.

---

## 3. What the business sells

Across the two locations there are roughly 150 sellable lines. They fall into four commercially different kinds of thing, and Lucy talks about each of them differently:

**Cooked food.** Made in the restaurant kitchen out of raw ingredients. There is no natural "purchase price" for a plate of mukimo — its cost comes from the ingredients consumed to produce it, and the relationship between the two is not formally measured. This is the hardest category to cost accurately, and Lucy knows it.

**Goods bought and resold as-is.** Sodas, water, biscuits, sweets, stationery, remedies, airtime. Bought as a unit, sold as a unit, with a clear margin between the two. This is straightforward retail and the great majority of what the canteen does.

**Services.** Printing, photocopying, binding, research, official forms — sold at the restaurant's printing station and the canteen's cyber corner. These carry no stock in the ordinary sense. They consume paper and toner and produce revenue, but there is nothing to count on a shelf at the end of the day.

**Packaging and consumables.** Takeaway containers, envelopes, cups, disposable cutlery. Some of this is genuinely sold to customers; much of it is simply a cost of trading that gets used up as food goes out the door.

These four behave differently enough that a single idea of "an item" will not serve all of them well.

---

## 4. How stock moves

Goods arrive from suppliers at the **restaurant's main store**. The store manager receives them and records the purchase.

From the store, raw ingredients are **issued to the kitchen**, and the store manager records how much was consumed in cooking. The kitchen turns those ingredients into meals. Some of that output stays on the restaurant floor to be sold; some is **sent across to the canteen**.

Crucially, **transfers run in both directions.** The restaurant sends prepared food and supplies to the canteen. The canteen sends printing and stationery stock back to the restaurant, because the restaurant's printing station needs it. These are two stocking points that supply each other, not a single supply line running one way.

Stock leaves the business through several routes, and Lucy distinguishes between them clearly:

- **Sold** — the normal case, at the till or as a delivery order.
- **Wasted or spoiled** — food that went off, or was ruined.
- **Consumed internally** — eaten or used by the business rather than sold.
- **Given away** — complimentary, to a guest or visitor.
- **Corrected** — a physical count didn't match the record, and the record was adjusted to reality.

Each of these is a different event with a different meaning, and Lucy wants them kept apart rather than lumped together as "missing."

### 4.1 Counting

Stock is counted **daily**. The count is done by Lucy herself or by the store manager — not by the cashiers. What should happen when a count disagrees with the record has not yet been decided.

Ingredients issued to the kitchen are not necessarily weighed or measured out, but **the kitchen does record how much of each ingredient was consumed each day.** Consumption is the number the business captures, rather than a formal issue-and-return.

How much goes across to the canteen each day is decided in the course of running the day; the basis for that decision has not been described.

### 4.2 Converting ingredients into food

Lucy raised something the current way of working does not do at all: she wants the system to understand **how raw ingredients convert into finished food.** Her example was potatoes into plates of chips — for some items, the business already knows roughly how much output a given quantity of input should produce.

Where that expected yield is known, she wants the conversion applied automatically rather than left to be worked out by hand. This has two uses: it gives cooked food a real cost derived from the ingredients that produced it, and it gives her an expectation to check reality against — if the potatoes should have produced a certain number of plates and didn't, that gap is worth seeing.

She was clear that this applies to *some* items, not all of them. Not everything the kitchen makes has a known, stable yield.

## 5. The people, and how a day runs

**Lucy — owner.** Sets prices, decides what's stocked, sees everything across both locations, and collects the money. Present irregularly, but when present, works any position.

**The store manager (Janiffer) — restaurant.** She owns the store end to end, and **she does not work the till.** Her responsibilities are receiving deliveries from suppliers and logging those purchases; issuing ingredients to the kitchen and recording what was consumed; and managing and recording what gets sent over to the canteen. The only selling she does is **recording delivery orders** — she does not handle counter sales.

**Two cashiers (Sarah, Mercy) — restaurant.** Their job is selling. Till sales through the day, delivery and takeaway orders, and recording any wastage they see on their watch.

**One attendant (Anne) — canteen.** She runs the canteen essentially single-handed: she sells, receives the transfers coming over from the restaurant, sends printing stock back the other way, and keeps her own location's stock records.

A day runs roughly like this. The store manager receives whatever suppliers deliver and issues ingredients to the kitchen. Cooking happens. The day's output is split between the restaurant floor and the canteen, and the transfer across is recorded. Both locations sell through the day — counter sales, plus delivery and pickup orders. Wastage, internal consumption and give-aways are noted as they occur. At close, the day's takings are counted. Then Lucy comes to collect.

### 5.1 Who worked, and paying them

Lucy wants to know **which staff worked on which day.** Not as a general interest — she pays her people **based on the number of days they have worked**, and she wants that calculation to come out of the same record rather than being reconstructed at the end of the month.

So there are two connected needs: a day-by-day record of who was working, and a payroll figure computed from it. What each person's pay is based on — a daily rate, a monthly figure spread across days worked, and how deductions or advances are handled — has not been described.

## 6. Money

Cashiers take money across the day, in **cash and by M-Pesa**. There is a paybill at the restaurant and a second one at the canteen. **Cash and M-Pesa are tracked separately** — they are not pooled into a single day's figure.

Some customers take goods **on credit** and settle later, so at any time there is money owed to the business by named customers. **Any member of staff may give credit, provided it is recorded, and any member of staff may record a repayment** — but the full picture of who owes what, across both locations, is Lucy's to see.

Delivery and pickup orders — which used to be arranged informally over WhatsApp with no record — are part of the day's takings too.

### 6.1 The end-of-day handover

**At the end of each day, Lucy personally receives the day's money from her staff.** This is a real, physical event and it is the point at which the business's records either hold up or don't.

She was explicit about what she needs from it: she wants to **record what each person actually handed over, and be told whether that matches what was expected** based on the sales recorded for that day. If someone hands over less than the day's sales say they should have, she wants that difference visible immediately — not discovered weeks later, and not left to her to work out in her head. Her word for the purpose was catching **mistakes**. This is a control, and it needs to work at the level of the individual person and the individual day.

**The canteen hands over daily as well**, on the same cycle as the restaurant.

### 6.2 Expected cash at hand

Beyond the daily handover, Lucy asked for something broader, and it is the single most significant gap in how the business is currently run.

She wants to know, at any moment, **how much cash she should be holding.** Her reasoning: money comes in when staff hand over the day's takings, and money goes back out when she spends it — buying stock from suppliers, paying for gas, charcoal, electricity and the rest. Every one of those payments should reduce the cash she is expected to be holding. What remains is the figure she can check her pocket against.

This works as a single balance because **she is the only person who spends the business's money.** Staff take money in; only Lucy pays it out.

She added one more case herself: if **she personally takes money out of the business** for her own use, that is not a business expense and shouldn't simply disappear. She wants it recorded as money **she owes the business**, so the expected figure stays truthful and she can see her own position against it.

Put plainly, Lucy is describing a **running cash book** — money in, money out, expected balance, verified against a physical count. It is a different question from profit. Profit asks whether the business earned; this asks where the money actually is. A business can be profitable and still have cash unaccounted for, and that is precisely the problem she is trying to solve.

## 7. What Lucy wants to see

Stated as her questions rather than as reports:

- **Am I making money?** For the day, the week, the month — per location and for the business overall. Real profit, after the cost of goods sold, operating expenses and wastage.
- **Did today add up?** What each cashier handed over against what the day's sales say it should have been.
- **How much cash should I be holding right now?** And how much of it have I taken out myself?
- **Who owes me?** Outstanding credit, by customer.
- **What is sitting in my stock, and what is it worth?** Across both locations.
- **What is running low?**
- **Who worked, and what am I paying them?** Days worked per person, and the pay that follows from it.
- **Who did what?** See §8.1.

## 8. The historical record

This is a requirement in its own right, separate from reporting, and Lucy is emphatic about it.

**Today she keeps this in Excel sheets,** and the structure of those sheets is exactly what she wants preserved. For any item, on any past day, she can look up: what she opened with, what was added, what was transferred in or out, what was sold, what was wasted or otherwise consumed, and what should therefore have been left at close. That is her mental model of her business, built up over years of running it, and it works.

What she wants is that same picture, for any item and any date, without maintaining it by hand — and with every movement attributable to the person who recorded it and the time they did. Nothing overwritten, nothing silently recalculated, the past readable exactly as it happened.

Any system that replaces the Excel sheets has to be at least as good as the Excel sheets at answering "what happened to this item that day." That is the bar.

### 8.1 An audit trail

Separately from the stock history, Lucy asked for an **audit trail**. She did not spell out exactly what she would use it for, but the intent behind it is consistent with everything else she asked for: she is an owner who is mostly not on site, and she wants to be able to see **who did what, and when**.

The distinction is worth keeping. The stock history in §8 answers *what happened to this item*. An audit trail answers *what did this person do* — including things that leave no trace in a stock figure at all, such as changing a price, editing an earlier entry, correcting a count, or writing off a debt. Where a record has been changed after the fact, she would want to see that it was changed, by whom, and what it was before.

What exactly she wants to be able to look up, and how far back, is still to be established.

## 9. Operating context and constraints

- **Staff work from their own phones**, on mobile data. Connectivity is not always reliable.
- **The people using it are not technical.** It has to be usable at speed, mid-service, with customers waiting, without training.
- **Kenyan context** — Kenyan Shillings, cash and M-Pesa side by side.
- **Every entry must be attributable** to the person who made it. This is not incidental; it is the basis of the handover control in §6.1.

## 10. Notes from discovery

Observations recorded during discovery, in no particular order.

- Bought-in goods have a clear purchase cost and a clear margin. Cooked food does not — its cost lies in the ingredients consumed to produce it. The yield conversion described in §4.2 is the business's own answer to this, for those items where an expected output is known.
- **Purchase costs fluctuate.** Lucy gave flour as an example: what she pays for it can change noticeably from one buying trip to the next. What a thing cost the business is therefore a moving figure rather than a fixed property of the item.
- Printing, photocopying and binding produce revenue but hold no stock in the ordinary sense. They consume paper and toner rather than being counted on a shelf.
- Packaging and consumables sit between being a sellable line and a cost of trading — some is charged to the customer, some is simply used up.
- The two locations supply each other rather than one supplying the other, so both hold stock and both send it out.
- Cash and M-Pesa are separate throughout — taken separately, tracked separately, handed over separately.
- Money is taken in by several people but paid out by one, which is what makes a single expected-cash figure meaningful.
- Credit can be extended and repaid by any member of staff, so the debtor record is written by many hands and read by Lucy.
- Lucy is mostly absent during trading hours but needs full operational capability when present.
- Staff record their own wastage and consumption, so those figures are self-reported.

## 11. Not yet established

Points that did not come up in discussion, or that were raised without enough detail to act on.

- What happens when a physical count disagrees with the record — whether the record is corrected, and who is allowed to correct it.
- How the daily quantity sent to the canteen is decided.
- The basis for staff pay: a daily rate, a monthly figure spread over days worked, and how advances or deductions are handled.
- What Lucy specifically wants to be able to look up in the audit trail, and how far back.
- Whether selling prices change as often as purchase costs do, and who is permitted to change them.
- Which items have a known, reliable yield for the ingredient-to-food conversion, and what those yields are.
- Whether cashiers hold a float overnight, or hand over everything.
- Whether any suppliers are paid on credit rather than on delivery.
- How printing is priced — per page, per job, or per document type.
